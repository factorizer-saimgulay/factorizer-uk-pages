What a differential-drive transporter means for your hall layout

An electric self-propelled transporter turns by driving one side against the other. What that means for aisle widths, turning space, floor loading and charging when a hall is planned around it.

Published
17 November 2026
Solution
Electric Self-Propelled Transporters

Our electric self-propelled transporters have no steering gear. The wheels on each side are driven, and the machine turns by driving one side faster than the other, or one side against the other. It is the arrangement that keeps the running gear simple and the deck low, and it gives the machine a particular way of moving that a hall should be planned around. This note is for whoever is laying out the building, the aisles and the charging points.

How it turns

With both sides driven forward at the same speed the machine runs straight. With the speeds different it follows a curve. With the sides driven against each other it rotates about its own centre and can change heading without moving forward at all. It cannot move sideways: a sideways displacement is made by turning, running, and turning back.

For planning, three movements cover everything the machine does: a straight run, a curve, and a turn on the spot.

The space a turn needs

When the machine turns on the spot, the corners of the deck sweep a circle whose radius is half the deck’s diagonal. The load on the deck sweeps a larger circle if it overhangs the deck, and for a long load the load’s envelope is the one that matters. As an example, a deck of 12 by 3.2 m has a half-diagonal of about 6.2 m; a rotor blade carried on a pair of such machines sweeps a circle set by the blade, not the deck, and the pair is driven so the blade pivots where the hall allows.

The width of a doorway, the clear height under a crane runway, and the distance between columns are set from the swept envelope at the places where the machine will turn, not from the deck size. Straight runs need less: the deck width, the load width if greater, and a margin on each side.

Floor loading

The gross weight is carried on the wheels, and the number of wheels is chosen so the load per wheel suits the floor. The configurator on this site works the axle count out from the deck size and the gross weight you set. On an existing floor, the question is the other way round: the floor’s bearing capacity decides the wheel load, and the machine is specified with enough wheels to stay under it. Pneumatic tyres spread the load over a larger contact area than a rail wheel; tracks spread it furthest of all and are chosen for ground rather than floors.

Floor surface

A differential-drive machine turns by scrubbing its wheels across the surface. Floors where the machine turns on the spot should be sound, flat and free of loose material: a joint or a step at the turning point is felt as a jolt through the load. On outdoor ground, tracks or large tyres are chosen so the surface can take the scrubbing.

Ramps and thresholds

The machine takes ramps within the grade its drive allows, and the specification states the maximum grade for the gross weight. Thresholds and drainage channels are crossed at right angles and slowly; where a route crosses them often, a ramp or a cover plate is part of the hall layout.

Charging

The transporter is battery electric. The battery is sized for the duty: the configurator proposes a pack for the deck and weight you set, or the largest pack the deck has room for. Charging is planned where the machine stands idle: the end of a shift, or the stand where the load is lifted off. A machine fitted for automatic charging docks on contacts at that point and needs no one to plug it in.

Remote control and where the operator stands

A remote-controlled machine is driven by an operator who walks with it. Sight lines matter: the layout should give the operator a place to stand at every turn from which both ends of the load can be seen. Lidar scanners, cameras and bumpers are available on the machine for protective stops; they add safety but do not replace the operator’s view.

A planning checklist

  • Routes: every place the machine runs, with the points where it turns.
  • The swept envelope of the longest load at each turning point.
  • Doorway widths and heights on the route, including under the crane.
  • Floor capacity and surface along the route; ground conditions outdoors.
  • Grades, thresholds and channels, and whether they are crossed often.
  • Where the machine waits, and where it charges.

With those, a machine is specified to the building rather than the other way round. Set the deck, the weight and the running gear in the transporter configurator to see the machine drawn to your figures, and read what the platform does on the transporters page.
